How to Calculate Profit Margins Correctly

Published September 4, 2026 · 5 min read

Profit margin and markup get confused constantly — and mixing them up leads to pricing mistakes that quietly eat into profitability. To calculate profit, subtract the relevant costs from revenue; to calculate profit margin, divide that profit by revenue and multiply by 100. This guide covers the gross-vs-net distinction and the margin-vs-markup mix-up that trips up many business owners.

The Profit Margin Formula

Profit = Revenue − Costs
Profit Margin = (Profit ÷ Revenue) × 100

Margin is expressed as a percentage of your selling price, not your cost — this distinction is exactly where markup confusion starts.

Gross Margin vs. Net Margin

These answer different questions:

  • Gross margin — revenue minus only the direct cost of producing the good or service (cost of goods sold). Shows production efficiency.
  • Net margin — revenue minus all expenses: production cost, operating costs, taxes, interest, overhead. Shows what you actually keep.

A business can have a healthy gross margin and a thin (or negative) net margin if overhead and operating costs are high — gross margin alone doesn't tell the full profitability story.

Worked Example

Product sells for: $100
Cost of goods: $60

Gross Margin = (100 − 60) ÷ 100 × 100 = 40%

40 cents of every dollar in revenue is gross profit, before accounting for overhead, marketing, and other operating costs.

Margin vs. Markup: The Mistake That Costs Money

This is where a lot of pricing goes wrong. Markup is profit as a percentage of cost, not selling price:

Markup = (Revenue − Cost) ÷ Cost × 100

Same transaction, two very different-looking numbers. If you price a product assuming "50% markup means 50% margin," you'll consistently underprice — a 50% markup on a $60 cost gives a $90 price, which is only a 33.3% margin, not 50%.

What's a Healthy Margin?

There is no universal target margin. The answer depends on the business model, accounting period, and which costs are included. Compare like with like: gross margin should not be compared directly with net margin, and industry comparisons are useful only when the definitions and reporting periods match.

Common Mistakes

  • Confusing margin and markup — this can lead to a selling price that does not produce the intended margin.
  • Only tracking gross margin — a healthy gross margin can hide a struggling net margin if overhead is high.
  • Ignoring hidden costs — payment processing fees, returns, and shipping quietly erode margin even when reported revenue looks strong.
  • Comparing margin across unrelated industries — a "good" margin varies enormously by business type.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between gross margin and net margin?
Gross margin only subtracts direct production cost. Net margin subtracts all business expenses, giving the full profitability picture.

What's the difference between margin and markup?
Margin is profit as a percentage of selling price. Markup is profit as a percentage of cost — the same transaction produces two different numbers.

What's a healthy profit margin?
There is no universal target. Compare the same type of margin using consistent cost definitions and accounting periods.

Why is my margin lower than expected even though sales are strong?
Rising costs, discounting, payment fees, shipping, and returns can quietly erode margin even when revenue looks healthy.

Should I price based on margin or markup?
Margin is generally more useful since it directly reflects what percentage of the sale price is actual profit.
