How to Calculate ROAS: Formula, Break-Even & Examples

Published September 2, 2026 · 6 min read

ROAS compares revenue attributed to advertising with the ad spend used to generate it. It is useful for comparing campaigns, but it does not measure full business profit by itself. This guide explains the formula, a gross-margin-based break-even estimate, and the assumptions that can make two ROAS figures difficult to compare.

The ROAS Formula

ROAS = Revenue from Ads ÷ Cost of Ads

Expressed as a ratio (4:1) or a percentage (400%). If you spent $1,000 on ads and those ads generated $4,000 in revenue, your ROAS is 4:1 — for every dollar spent, you got four dollars back in revenue.

Worked Example

Campaign: $2,500 spent on Facebook ads over one week

Revenue attributed to those ads: $9,750
ROAS = 9,750 ÷ 2,500 = 3.9 (or 390%)

Sounds solid at a glance — nearly 4x your ad spend back in revenue. But whether that's actually profitable depends entirely on your margin, which is where most people stop calculating and start guessing.

What Is a Good ROAS?

There is no universal ROAS target. The number depends on gross margin, other variable costs, attribution, and the business objective. Start by estimating the break-even point using a margin that reflects the relevant non-ad costs, then account separately for any costs that the margin excludes. A campaign above a simplified break-even estimate is not automatically profitable.

How to Calculate Break-Even ROAS

Break-Even ROAS ≈ 1 ÷ Gross Margin (as a decimal)

If the gross margin available to cover advertising is 25%, the simplified break-even estimate is 1 ÷ 0.25 = 4.0x. This assumes the margin includes the relevant non-ad variable costs. If shipping, payment fees, returns, taxes, or other costs are excluded, the actual break-even ROAS will be higher.

ROAS vs. ROI: What's the Difference?

These get used interchangeably, but they answer different questions:

  • ROAS — revenue ÷ ad spend only. Tells you how efficiently ads generated revenue.
  • ROI — (total return − total investment) ÷ total investment × 100%. It aims to compare profit with the full investment base, which can include costs beyond ads.

A campaign can have an impressive ROAS and a negative ROI at the same time, if the product's margin is thin enough. ROAS measures ad efficiency; ROI measures actual profitability.

Common Mistakes When Calculating ROAS

  • Mixing ROAS and margin into one number. Keep them separate — calculate ROAS first, then check it against your break-even ROAS.
  • Including non-ad costs in the denominator. Only actual ad spend belongs there — not shipping, taxes, or platform fees unrelated to the ads themselves.
  • Using a generic ROAS benchmark without checking your own costs. A target depends on the margin and costs behind the attributed revenue.
  • Attributing all revenue to the last ad clicked. Multi-touch attribution is a deeper topic, but be aware that simple last-click ROAS can overstate a single channel's actual impact.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good ROAS?
There is no universal target. Compare the campaign with a break-even estimate based on your own margin and relevant costs, then consider attribution and business goals.

What's the difference between ROAS and ROI?
ROAS measures revenue per dollar of ad spend. ROI measures profit relative to total investment, including product cost and overhead.

How do I calculate break-even ROAS?
As a simplified estimate, divide 1 by the gross-margin fraction available to cover advertising. At 25%, that is 4.0x, before any omitted costs.

Does ROAS account for profit margin?
No. ROAS compares revenue with ad spend. The planner separately estimates a break-even ROAS from the entered gross margin, but that estimate does not automatically include other costs.

Should I include taxes or shipping in ad spend?
ROAS uses the ad spend paid to the platform in its denominator. Include other relevant costs when estimating margin or full profitability; do not silently treat revenue minus ad spend as net profit.
