Understanding SaaS Metrics: CAC, LTV & Payback

Published September 5, 2026 · 6 min read

CAC, LTV, and payback are useful summaries of acquisition cost and customer economics, but each depends on how inputs are defined. This guide distinguishes common SaaS formulas from the simplified assumptions in FinCalcHub Marketing’s calculator.

Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)

Calculator CAC = Total Marketing Spend ÷ New Customers Acquired

The FinCalcHub Marketing calculator divides the entered total marketing spend by new customers acquired. A broader company CAC analysis may also allocate sales salaries, marketing tools, content production, and other acquisition costs; those amounts affect this calculator only if included in the spend input. Use matching time periods for spend and customer counts.

Customer Lifetime Value (LTV)

Calculator LTV = (Monthly Revenue per Customer − Monthly Operating Cost per Customer) × Entered Lifespan in Months

This is the simplified formula used by the FinCalcHub Marketing calculator. It uses the entered average customer lifespan directly and subtracts the entered monthly operating cost per customer from monthly revenue per customer. Its visible churn-rate field is not used in the calculation. Other SaaS LTV models may infer lifespan from churn or apply a more detailed gross-margin and retention model; those formulas can produce different results and should not be confused with this tool’s output.

Worked Example

Total marketing spend: $80,000
New customers: 100
Monthly revenue per customer: $100
Monthly operating cost per customer: $20
Entered lifespan: 50 months

CAC = $80,000 ÷ 100 = $800
LTV = ($100 − $20) × 50 = $4,000
LTV:CAC = $4,000 ÷ $800 = 5:1
Simple payback = $800 ÷ $80 per month = 10 months

The calculator’s churn input does not change these results.

How to Interpret LTV:CAC

There is no universal healthy ratio. Interpret LTV:CAC alongside the definitions used for CAC, gross margin or operating cost, customer retention, payback, and the period in which costs and customers were measured. A high ratio based on optimistic or mismatched assumptions is not proof that a growth model is sustainable.

CAC Payback Period

Calculator payback (months) = CAC ÷ (Monthly Revenue per Customer − Monthly Operating Cost per Customer)

In the example, $800 ÷ ($100 − $20) = 10 months. This simplified estimate assumes the entered monthly net revenue continues; it does not model churn, expansion, contract timing, discounts, taxes, or changes in cost. There is no universal payback target for every SaaS business.

Check the Formula Before Comparing LTV

Many LTV models estimate customer lifespan from observed retention or churn. This calculator instead multiplies monthly net revenue per customer by the lifespan entered by the user; changing the visible churn-rate field does not affect the result. Review the formula and compare the entered lifespan with observed cohort data before using the estimate in a decision.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good LTV:CAC ratio?
There is no universal target. Interpret the ratio using consistent definitions for CAC, LTV, retention, margin, and measurement periods.

What counts as customer acquisition cost?
It depends on the analysis. This calculator divides its total-marketing-spend input by new customers; a fuller company analysis may allocate additional acquisition expenses to that input.

How is customer lifetime value calculated?
This calculator uses (monthly revenue per customer − monthly operating cost per customer) × entered customer lifespan in months. The displayed churn field is not used. Churn-based models are different.

What is CAC payback period and why does it matter?
It estimates how many months of the entered monthly net revenue per customer are needed to match CAC. This calculator does not model churn or changes in revenue and cost over time.

Can an apparently strong LTV:CAC ratio still be misleading?
Yes. A ratio can look strong if the entered lifespan is too optimistic, CAC omits acquisition costs, or the measures use mismatched periods. Compare assumptions with observed customer data.
