Compound Interest Explained: Formula, Contributions & Example

Published September 2, 2026 · 5 min read

Compound interest applies each period’s return to the original principal and the interest already accumulated. The formulas make it possible to explore how time, rate, compounding frequency, and contributions affect a projection, but they do not predict actual investment returns.

The Compound Interest Formula

A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n×t)

Here A is the future value, P is the starting principal, r is the annual rate as a decimal, n is the number of compounding periods per year, and t is the number of years. The principal-only formula does not include regular contributions. Simple Interest vs. Compound Interest

Simple interest is calculated only on your original principal — it grows at a flat, constant rate every year. Compound interest is calculated on your principal plus all interest already earned, so the base it's calculated on keeps growing, which means the growth curve accelerates over time instead of staying flat.

Worked Example: Principal Plus Monthly Contributions

Starting principal: $10,000
Monthly contribution: $500
Assumed annual rate: 7%, compounded monthly for 10 years

Using a monthly rate of 0.07 ÷ 12 and 120 monthly periods, the calculator estimates a future value of approximately $106,639. Total entered contributions are $70,000, so the modeled growth is approximately $36,639.

This is an illustration using a constant assumed rate and month-end contributions. It excludes taxes, fees, and changing returns; actual account values may be higher or lower.

Does Compounding Frequency Matter?

For the same nominal annual rate and time period, more frequent compounding can change the mathematical result. The size of the difference depends on the rate, duration, and contribution timing. Compounding frequency does not make a constant-rate projection a forecast of actual returns.

Compound Interest Works Against You Too

The same math that builds wealth in a savings account works against you in debt. Credit card balances typically compound, which is why an unpaid balance can grow faster than expected — you're paying interest on interest, the same mechanism that grows your investments, just running in the opposite direction.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does compound interest work?
Each period’s interest is calculated on the principal plus previously accumulated interest. A contribution calculator also needs an assumption for when contributions are made.

What's the difference between compound and simple interest?
Simple interest only applies to your original principal and grows at a flat rate. Compound interest applies to principal plus accumulated interest, so growth accelerates over time.

Does compounding frequency actually matter?
It changes the mathematical result for a given nominal rate, but its effect depends on the inputs. The assumed rate and time period also matter, and none of these inputs guarantees an investment outcome.

Why can starting earlier affect a projection?
Earlier contributions have more periods in which to compound, all else equal. The result still depends on the assumed rate, contribution schedule, fees, taxes, and actual future performance.

Does compound interest apply to debt too?
Yes — credit card debt and some loans compound the same way, which is why unpaid balances can grow faster than expected.
